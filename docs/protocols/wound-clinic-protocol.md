# Wound Clinic Protocol: Baseline Investigations and Micronutrient Support

Nov 26, 2026 · provided by the practice owner for encoding in AstroHEALTH (stage 5)

## Purpose and scope

Every new wound patient gets the same baseline panel. Micronutrients are given to correct a deficiency or to cover high-risk patients, not as a routine cocktail for everyone.

- **Applies to:** adults and children seen in the wound clinic with acute or chronic wounds, burns, ulcers or pressure injuries.
- **How to use it:** do the baseline panel at the first visit, add targeted tests by wound type, then follow the monitoring schedule.
- **Doses:** all doses are by mouth unless stated, and mineral doses are elemental (for example, mg of elemental zinc, not mg of zinc sulphate).
- **Local check:** confirm brand strengths against the products stocked in the clinic pharmacy, as Nigerian brands vary.

## Baseline investigations (every new patient)

These tests find the common reasons wounds fail to heal: anaemia, uncontrolled diabetes, infection, renal or liver disease, and malnutrition.

| Test | Why it matters for healing | Target or action point |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Full blood count | Anaemia reduces oxygen delivery; white cells flag infection | Hb at least 10 g/dL (adult); investigate if lower |
| ESR and CRP | Infection, osteomyelitis, inflammation; also needed to interpret zinc and ferritin | Trend over time; persistently high CRP suggests deep infection |
| Random blood sugar and HbA1c | Hyperglycaemia impairs neutrophils and collagen | HbA1c below 7% (53 mmol/mol) where safe |
| Electrolytes, urea, creatinine | Renal function guides protein load, drug and mineral dosing | Adjust protein and zinc if eGFR is low |
| Liver function tests with albumin | Liver disease; albumin reflects inflammation and illness more than nutrition | Do not use albumin alone to diagnose malnutrition |
| Serum ferritin and transferrin saturation | Iron deficiency (ferritin rises with inflammation) | Ferritin below 30 ng/mL, or below 100 ng/mL with high CRP, suggests iron deficiency |
| Serum zinc (fasting, morning) | Deficiency delays epithelialisation | Below about 70 mcg/dL (10.7 micromol/L); falsely low when CRP is high |
| 25-OH vitamin D | Deficiency is common and affects immunity | Below 20 ng/mL (50 nmol/L) = deficient |
| HIV screening (with consent) | Immunosuppression, atypical or slow wounds | Refer for care if positive |
| Haemoglobin electrophoresis (genotype) | Sickle cell disease causes leg ulcers and poor healing | Especially leg ulcers in young adults and children |
| Urinalysis | Glucose, protein, infection | Follow up abnormal results |
| Nutrition screen: weight, height, BMI or weight-for-age, MUAC | Identifies malnutrition early | Use MUST (adults) or STRONGkids (children) |
| Wound baseline: size (length × width × depth), photo, tissue type, exudate | The reference for measuring progress | Measure and photograph at every visit the same way |

## Targeted investigations

Add these when the wound type or the clinical picture calls for them.

| Situation | Investigation |
| --- | --- |
| Clinically infected wound | Deep tissue culture or curettage after cleansing (better than a surface swab); blood culture if febrile |
| Probe-to-bone, exposed bone, or non-healing diabetic foot | Plain X-ray; MRI if available; bone biopsy for culture and histology |
| Leg or foot ulcer | Ankle–brachial pressure index (ABPI) before compression; arterial Doppler if ABPI below 0.8 or above 1.3 |
| Suspected venous disease | Venous duplex scan |
| Chronic ulcer over 3 months, rolled or heaped edges, or unusual look | Wedge biopsy from the edge to exclude malignancy (including Marjolin's ulcer in old burn scars) |
| Suspected vasculitis or autoimmune ulcer | ANA, rheumatoid factor, ANCA, complement, urine protein |
| Large burns or prolonged poor intake | Magnesium, phosphate, calcium; prealbumin where available |
| Suspected B12 or folate deficiency (macrocytosis, glossitis, poor diet) | Serum B12 and folate |
| Suspected copper deficiency (anaemia or neutropenia on long-term zinc) | Serum copper and caeruloplasmin |
| Suspected thyroid disease | TSH |
| Children with poor growth | Weight and height plotted on growth charts; stool for parasites and blood film for malaria where relevant |

## Monitoring schedule

A wound that has not shrunk by about 40–50% in area after 4 weeks of good care is unlikely to heal on the current plan. Reassess the cause, look for infection or ischaemia, and consider biopsy.

| When | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| Every visit | Measure and photograph the wound; check for infection; record weight in children |
| Every 2 weeks | Calculate % change in area from baseline |
| Week 4 | Decision point: if under 40–50% smaller, re-investigate and change the plan |
| Every 4–6 weeks | FBC and CRP if anaemic or infected; blood sugar log for diabetics |
| Every 3 months | HbA1c (diabetics); ferritin and Hb after starting iron; vitamin D after a loading course |
| After 8–12 weeks of zinc | Repeat serum zinc; stop zinc once the wound has epithelialised |
| Children | Weight every visit; height monthly; plot both on growth charts |

## Protein and calories come first

No vitamin makes up for too little protein and energy. For malnourished adults with a pressure injury, international guidance recommends 30–35 kcal/kg/day and 1.25–1.5 g protein/kg/day ([RNAO summary of NPIAP/EPUAP](https://bpgmobile.rnao.ca/node/1257)).

| Group | Energy | Protein | Fluid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Adult, chronic wound or malnourished | 30–35 kcal/kg/day | 1.25–1.5 g/kg/day; up to 2 g/kg for large or deep wounds | About 1 mL/kcal/day |
| Adult, major burn | Specialist estimate (often higher) | 1.5–2 g/kg/day | Per burn protocol |
| Child, wound or malnourished | Age-based needs plus 10–20% | About 1.5–2.5 g/kg/day (higher in infants and burns) | Per weight (Holliday–Segar) |

- **Local foods that help:** eggs, beans and moi moi, fish (including crayfish and stockfish), chicken, milk, groundnuts, and soya.
- **Oral supplements:** use a high-protein drink between meals if intake stays poor; arginine-containing formulas (about 4.5 g arginine a day) are suggested for pressure injuries.
- **Kidney disease:** reduce protein if eGFR is low and not on dialysis; involve the physician or dietitian.

## Vitamins and minerals: adults (18 years and over)

Give every chronic-wound patient a daily multivitamin-mineral. Add a therapeutic dose only for a proven or strongly suspected deficiency, and stop when it is corrected or the wound heals. Evidence that extra zinc or vitamin C speeds healing in people who are not deficient is weak.

| Nutrient | Who gets it | Adult dose | Duration and notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Multivitamin with minerals | All chronic wounds, poor intake, elderly | 1 tablet daily (RDA-level) | Until healed and eating well |
| Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) | Poor diet, smokers, pressure injuries, suspected deficiency | 500 mg daily (250 mg twice daily) | Until healed; reduce in kidney stones or severe renal disease |
| Zinc (elemental) | Low serum zinc, malnutrition, large exuding wounds, burns | 25–50 mg daily with food (e.g. zinc sulphate 220 mg = 50 mg elemental) | Usually 8–12 weeks, then stop; long courses cause copper deficiency |
| Iron (elemental) | Iron-deficiency anaemia | 65 mg once daily or on alternate days (e.g. ferrous sulphate 200 mg) | Take with vitamin C, not with tea or antacids; continue 3 months after Hb normalises |
| Folic acid | Folate deficiency, sickle cell disease, macrocytosis | 5 mg daily | 4 months for deficiency; continuous in sickle cell disease. Check B12 first |
| Vitamin B12 | Proven B12 deficiency | Hydroxocobalamin 1 mg IM three times a week for 2 weeks, then every 3 months | Lifelong if pernicious anaemia |
| Vitamin D3 | Level below 20 ng/mL | 50,000 IU once weekly for 8 weeks, then 800–2,000 IU daily | Check calcium; caution in sarcoidosis or high calcium |
| Vitamin A | Patients on steroids with poor healing; proven deficiency | 10,000–25,000 IU daily for 10–14 days only | NOT in pregnancy or women who may be pregnant (max 10,000 IU); avoid in liver disease |
| Arginine | Pressure injury stage 2 or worse, malnourished | About 4.5 g daily (in a specialised drink) | Avoid in sepsis |

Tolerable upper limits for long-term use in adults: zinc 40 mg, vitamin C 2,000 mg, vitamin A 10,000 IU, vitamin D 4,000 IU daily. The short, monitored therapeutic courses above may exceed these.

## Vitamins and minerals: children (1 month to 17 years)

Dose children by weight, never above the adult dose, and check the product strength in mg per 5 mL before prescribing. Neonates and preterm infants need neonatal or paediatric input.

| Nutrient | Who gets it | Child dose | Duration and notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Paediatric multivitamin | All chronic wounds, burns, poor intake | Age dose on the product label | Until healed and eating well |
| Vitamin C | Poor diet, burns, suspected deficiency | Under 4 years: 100–125 mg daily. 4–11 years: 250 mg daily. 12–17 years: 500 mg daily | Until healed |
| Zinc (elemental) | Low serum zinc, malnutrition, diarrhoea, burns | 0.5–1 mg/kg once daily (maximum 40 mg/day) | 8–12 weeks, then stop; give with food |
| Iron (elemental) | Iron-deficiency anaemia (low Hb and low ferritin) | 3 mg/kg once daily; up to 6 mg/kg/day in severe anaemia (maximum 200 mg/day). Prevention: 1–2 mg/kg daily | 3 months after Hb normalises. Treat malaria and worms first; avoid during acute severe infection |
| Folic acid | Folate deficiency; sickle cell disease | Under 1 year: 500 mcg/kg daily (maximum 5 mg). 1 year and over: 5 mg daily (sickle cell maintenance per local protocol, often 2.5–5 mg daily) | Check B12 before long courses |
| Vitamin D3 | Level below 20 ng/mL or rickets | Under 1 year: 2,000 IU daily. 1–12 years: 3,000–6,000 IU daily. Over 12 years: 6,000 IU daily | 12 weeks, then 400 IU (under 1 year) or 600 IU daily. Give with calcium if intake is poor |
| Vitamin A | Only per WHO schedule, or proven deficiency or measles | 6–11 months: 100,000 IU single dose. 12 months and over: 200,000 IU single dose (every 4–6 months if not already given) | Do not give extra vitamin A only to speed wound healing in children; overdose is toxic |
| Vitamin B12 | Proven deficiency | Hydroxocobalamin 1 mg IM, with specialist advice | Specialist follow-up |

## Cautions and when to stop

- **Zinc:** long-term high doses cause copper deficiency, anaemia and low immunity ([AAFP](https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2009/0501/p768.html)). Stop at healing or after 12 weeks, and give zinc and iron at different times.
- **Iron:** keep out of children's reach (overdose is dangerous); stools turn black; avoid in thalassaemia or iron overload; do not give with tea, milk or antacids.
- **Vitamin A:** teratogenic. Avoid high doses in pregnancy and in women who may be pregnant; avoid in liver disease.
- **Vitamin C:** high doses can cause kidney stones; avoid large doses in G6PD deficiency, iron overload and severe renal disease.
- **Vitamin D:** stop if calcium rises; caution in kidney stones and sarcoidosis.
- **Renal impairment:** reduce protein, avoid high-dose vitamin C and vitamin A, and review zinc.
- **Pregnancy and breastfeeding:** use a standard antenatal supplement and folic acid; avoid high-dose vitamin A.
- **Sickle cell disease:** iron only for proven iron deficiency (patients may be iron-loaded from transfusions).
